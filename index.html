Molecular & clinical pharmacology

Antipsychotics & the D2 receptor

Almost every antipsychotic acts at the dopamine D2 receptor, a class A G-protein-coupled receptor that signals through Gαi/o. First-generation agents are competitive antagonists; most second-generation agents combine D2 antagonism with 5-HT2A blockade; and the newest class are D2 partial agonists that occupy the receptor while leaving part of its signaling intact. Because antagonism is competitive, occupancy depends on the local dopamine concentration, which differs between the dopaminergic projections - the reason one plasma concentration produces a therapeutic effect in one pathway and adverse effects in another. This resource integrates receptor structure, binding profiles, drug properties and pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic models.

Receptor targets & clinical effects

Antipsychotics are not selective: each binds a characteristic set of aminergic receptors, and the order of its affinities determines which effects appear at therapeutic doses. Blockade of D2 underlies the antipsychotic effect, extrapyramidal symptoms and prolactin elevation; blockade of 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C, H1, M1 and α1 accounts for much of the remaining clinical profile. Pharmacodynamics

Competitive binding at D2 between drug (concentration C, dissociation constant Ki) and dopamine ([DA], KDA): the fractional occupancy is θ = (C/Ki) / (1 + C/Ki + [DA]/KDA), and receptor signaling is E = ([DA]/KDA + α·C/Ki) / (1 + C/Ki + [DA]/KDA), where α is the intrinsic activity of the drug (α = 0 for an antagonist, 0 < α < 1 for a partial agonist). The functional blockade that drives clinical effect is therefore θ·(1 − α), which is why a partial agonist can occupy most receptors without abolishing signaling.

Pharmacokinetics

One-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination, including chains of active metabolites (9-hydroxyrisperidone after risperidone, dehydroaripiprazole after aripiprazole, the two sequential metabolites of cariprazine) and patient- and interaction-related modifiers acting on specific elimination pathways (CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, glucuronidation, renal excretion). Depot injections are absorption-limited, so their apparent half-life is set by release from the injection site rather than by elimination.

Biotransformation & active metabolites

Most antipsychotics undergo hepatic oxidation by CYP1A2, CYP2D6 or CYP3A4, several by direct glucuronidation, and two (paliperidone, amisulpride) are largely excreted unchanged by the kidney. Active metabolites with half-lives longer than the parent drug determine how long dose changes take to take full effect. These pathways determine the modifier effects simulated above.

Safety & monitoring

The adverse-effect burden of this class follows directly from the receptor profiles in Receptor targets and the exposure in Pharmacokinetics. Baseline and periodic monitoring is recommended for all agents, with drug-specific additions.

Monitoring commonly recommended

  • Metabolic. Weight and waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose or HbA1c and a lipid profile at baseline, at 3 months, then at least annually; more often with clozapine, olanzapine and quetiapine, and in children and adolescents, in whom weight gain is greater.
  • Movement disorders. Examination for parkinsonism, akathisia, acute dystonia and tardive dyskinesia at baseline and at each review (for example with the AIMS or a comparable scale), particularly with first-generation agents and higher D2 occupancy.
  • Prolactin. Measure when symptoms suggest elevation (menstrual disturbance, galactorrhea, sexual dysfunction, gynecomastia); highest with amisulpride, risperidone, paliperidone and first-generation agents.
  • Cardiac. ECG before and during treatment where QTc prolongation is a concern (high-dose or intravenous haloperidol, ziprasidone, pimozide, amisulpride, or co-prescribed QT-prolonging drugs), and with risk factors or electrolyte disturbance.
  • Clozapine-specific. Absolute neutrophil count on a mandatory schedule; troponin and C-reactive protein if myocarditis is suspected in the first 8 weeks; active enquiry about constipation; plasma levels when efficacy, adherence, smoking change or infection is in question.
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome. A rare, potentially fatal reaction (fever, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, raised creatine kinase) requiring immediate withdrawal of the drug and supportive treatment.

FDA boxed warning

Increased mortality in older adults with dementia-related psychosis. Older adults with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotic drugs are at an increased risk of death. Analyses of placebo-controlled trials of atypical antipsychotics in this population showed a risk of death in drug-treated patients of between 1.6 and 1.7 times that in placebo-treated patients; conventional antipsychotics carry a comparable or greater risk. No antipsychotic is approved for the treatment of dementia-related psychosis except brexpiprazole for agitation associated with Alzheimer dementia, and pimavanserin for hallucinations and delusions of Parkinson disease psychosis.

Antidepressant-class warnings additionally apply to the agents licensed as adjuncts in major depressive disorder (aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, quetiapine XR, olanzapine-fluoxetine), which carry the boxed warning on suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children, adolescents and young adults.

Notes & limitations
  • Parameter values. Receptor affinities (Ki) are taken from section 12.2 of the FDA label where it states them, and otherwise are the ChEMBL median for human receptors, compared with the IUPHAR/BPS Guide to Pharmacology; reported values for the same drug and receptor often differ between laboratories by 3-10-fold. Half-lives, volumes of distribution, protein binding and bioavailability come from the FDA labels (section 12.3) where available. The source of each value, the value reported there and its status (consistent, calibrated, model assumption or not yet verified) are listed in the searchable data tables; the older generic labels of haloperidol, chlorpromazine and fluphenazine contain no pharmacokinetic data, so those values are not yet sourced.
  • Occupancy model. Occupancy follows mass-action binding at equilibrium. Dopamine is treated as a single competing concentration per pathway, with one effective KDA; in reality receptors exist in high- and low-affinity states, dopamine fluctuates phasically on a millisecond-to-second scale, and agonist affinity depends on G-protein coupling. Receptor reserve, internalization and up-regulation during chronic treatment (relevant to dopamine supersensitivity and tardive dyskinesia) are not represented.
  • Dose-occupancy calibration. Each drug carries one parameter (the plasma concentration of the D2-active moiety giving 50 % striatal D2 occupancy) fitted to published PET studies, mostly with 11C-raclopride, so that the model reproduces the reported occupancy at the studied doses (for example clozapine 75-900 mg/day: 16-69 % in the model, 16-68 % reported). It conflates plasma and brain concentrations and ignores active transport at the blood-brain barrier - which is why amisulpride, risperidone and paliperidone raise prolactin more than their striatal occupancy would suggest: the pituitary lies outside the barrier. Different radioligands give different values; for lurasidone, a 18F-fallypride study in patients suggests markedly lower occupancy than the 11C-raclopride study used here.
  • Occupancy thresholds. The 65 %, 72 % and 78 % thresholds for antipsychotic response, prolactin elevation and extrapyramidal symptoms are group-level findings from small PET studies of mainly first-generation agents (Kapur et al. 2000; Farde et al. 1992) and vary between individuals. Clozapine and quetiapine are effective at considerably lower striatal occupancy, which the thresholds do not explain; rapid dissociation from D2 (Kapur & Seeman 2001) and contributions from other receptors have been proposed as reasons.
  • Partial agonism. Intrinsic activity is modeled as a single number per drug and receptor, applied to one signaling output. In reality it is pathway-dependent (G-protein versus β-arrestin, functional selectivity) and varies with receptor density and the tissue measured, so the values here describe a tendency rather than a fixed quantity.
  • Pharmacokinetic model. One compartment with first-order absorption and elimination; distribution phases are therefore absent. Absorption rate constants were fitted to the time to peak in the FDA labels; as an independent check, the model predicts a steady-state peak of ≈ 306 ng/mL for clozapine 100 mg twice daily, against 319 ng/mL in the label. Depot formulations are modeled as a single first-order release process, which reproduces the apparent half-life but not the timing of the peak (e.g. ≈ 5 rather than 13 days for paliperidone palmitate). Concentrations of the parent drug and its D2-active metabolites are summed without adjustment for differing affinity, and only metabolites flagged as D2-active enter the sum.
  • Receptor structure. The displayed structure is a thermostabilized crystal construct with a fused lysozyme in place of intracellular loop 3 (removed here) and several stabilizing mutations; the extracellular loops and the amino terminus are partly unresolved. Drugs other than risperidone, haloperidol and dopamine are shown on the risperidone pose, so their binding geometry is not modeled. The inactive-to-active morph interpolates linearly between two experimental structures and is not a simulation of the activation pathway; the lipid bilayer is drawn schematically.
  • Regional distribution. Abundance levels are a qualitative summary of receptor autoradiography, in situ hybridization and PET, partly in rodent brain. Levels are relative within each receptor and not comparable in absolute density between receptors. Each structure is shaded uniformly, so laminar and nuclear differences (for example 5-HT2A concentrated in cortical layer V, or D3 in the nucleus accumbens shell) appear only in the descriptions. The choroid plexus, which carries the highest 5-HT2C density, is not part of the brain model.
  • Clinical scope. Dose equivalents, monitoring recommendations and the summarized indications are teaching simplifications; they differ between national formularies and guidelines and are not a substitute for a current label or guideline.
